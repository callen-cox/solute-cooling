The problem: escape to space

Habitat ships need fast, reliable links, so communication relays orbit the sun between them. Each relay's servers make heat, and in space there is no air or water to carry it away: heat can only leave as infrared light from a radiator (Q = εσAT⁴). Radiators sized for the worst case are big and heavy, and at low load the water inside them can freeze. This watchdog runs the cooling autonomously, safely and with as little hardware as possible.

Three tiers, with a watchdog above them

Tier 1 · Radiators A pumped water loop carries server heat to radiator panels that face away from the sun, coordinated with the flight computer that points the solar arrays at it.
Tier 2 · Wax PCM When the radiators alone can't keep up, a valve routes the loop through n-eicosane wax, which absorbs 247 kJ/kg as it melts at 36.6 °C. Spare radiator capacity re-freezes it later, ready for the next surge.
Tier 3 · Go down safely If that still isn't enough, the watchdog sheds load to 50%, then shuts the servers down, with the radiators stowed behind their shield if the sun is on them, until it cools.
Freeze guard At low load it stows the radiators and warms the loop with the cheapest heat first: the wax's stored heat, then a pass past the sun-facing side, and only then an electric heater.

Every step (every 5 s of relay time)

  1. Sense: three sensors are median-voted, so a broken one is outvoted. Each is also compared with the others over about 30 seconds, so one that slowly drifts is dropped before it can fool the vote. A reading the water can't physically produce, like a radiation glitch, is ignored. With no usable data it assumes the worst and shuts down.
  2. Forecast: an energy balance (server heat + absorbed sunlight = radiated heat) gives the temperature the water would settle at with the radiators fully out. Above the wax's melting point, the radiators can't keep up on their own. The trend is also projected 5 minutes ahead.
  3. Supervise: the ladders above, for the coolant and the hottest chip. Everything wrong right now is listed in the alerts panel, under a flashing red warning sign, most serious first.
  4. Act: the mode sets the radiators, the wax valve and the freeze guard (below).

The watchdog: what makes it safe

  • A forecast alone never shuts the relay down. Forecasts shed load early. A shutdown needs a real reading over the limit, or a forecast that still says too hot after load was shed.
  • It watches the chips, not just the water. A clogged cold plate can cook a chip while the coolant looks fine, so it projects the chips' trend, sheds load before they reach their limit, and won't go back to full power while that would overheat them.
  • It catches bad sensors, both sudden failures (outvoted) and slow drift (dropped before it matters). If the chip sensor itself fails, it holds the load at half until it's back.
  • It survives radiation. Out in solar orbit there's no planet's magnetic field to block it, so readings and memory get corrupted now and then. One-off impossible readings are ignored, and the safety limits are kept in three spare copies that out-vote a flipped bit every step. Try the ☢ Radiation storm.
  • It watches the pump. In free fall the water only moves while a pump drives it. If the flow stops, pump B takes over at once; if both stop, the servers go down within seconds.
  • It doesn't flap. Load stays shed for at least 5 minutes, and until full power would fit again. After a shutdown it stays off at least 2 minutes and restarts at half load.
  • It fails safe. With no trustworthy reading it shuts the servers down and stows the radiators, and a survival heater with its own hardware thermostat keeps the water liquid. If the control software crashes, it cuts server power. If it freezes, its heartbeat stops and a hardware watchdog cuts the power after 30 s.

The physics, honestly

  • In orbit, hot water doesn't rise. Convection needs gravity, so the loop has a small pump. A loop heat pipe, which moves its fluid with a wick, would do it with no pump at all.
  • Radiators shed heat in proportion to their area, so they are thin panels, not tanks. Sizing them for the average load and letting the wax absorb the peaks is what saves material: see the Sizing tab.
  • Water expands about 9% when it freezes and can split pipes, which is why the freeze guard matters.
  • Orbiting the sun, there is no planet shadow, so the sunlight is constant. A close pass (3× the sunlight) is the relay swinging nearer the sun.

Where it runs

  • On a laptop: start.bat runs the Python control loop as a FastAPI server, and this page streams from it.
  • Online: with no server, this page loads Python into your browser (Pyodide, WebAssembly) and runs the exact same coolant/core.py.
  • On real hardware: swap the simulator for a HAL class that reads real sensors and drives the real valves, radiator deployment, heater and server power caps, alongside the flight computer.
